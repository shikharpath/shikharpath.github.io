01 / Cultural reportage

The Illusion of Time in Chandni Chowk

Where the 1800s and 2025 coexist · By Shikhar Pathak

Some places belong to history books and places that belong to tomorrow. And then there is Chandni Chowk—a place that refuses to belong to any single time at all. Walk through its lanes, and you may find yourself in two centuries at once, the past lingering like the scent of ittar, the present flashing by in the neon glow of digital signboards.

A few weeks ago, I stood at a tea stall near Fatehpuri Masjid, sipping from a scalding kulhad, when an old man beside me remarked, “Beta, yeh sadak ab bhi waise hi hai jaise jabaanwalon ke zamaane mein thi.” (The road, unchanged since the jabaanwalas’ time.) Who were the jabaanwalas? He didn’t say. He simply stirred his tea and turned back to his stories. That’s the thing about Chandni Chowk—it does not give you answers, only hints.

A Market That Collects Time

The streets of Chandni Chowk are a contradiction that makes perfect sense. At one end, a shopkeeper in Dariba Kalan weighs gold earrings on a rusting scale, his fingers stained with the touch of generations before him. A few feet away, another jeweller offers “pure 22-carat” on an Instagram Live, his phone held steady by a ring light clipped onto his counter. A trader in Khari Baoli closes a deal with a head nod—no paper, no signature, only trust—while the man next to him verifies a transaction on Google Pay.

You could call this coexistence. But I wonder—does time truly move here? Or does it simply collect, like dust settling on an antique bookshelf, layer over layer, past and present woven so tightly that neither can be undone?

The Music of Two Centuries

Close your eyes, and the soundscape of Chandni Chowk will tell you its own story. The clinking of copper pots, the rhythmic crackle of frying samosas, the echoes of azaan from Jama Masjid—all of it merges with the modern hum of car horns and buzzing mobile notifications. Somewhere in the crowd, a qawwali floats from an old speaker, its melody swallowed by the honk of a passing e-rickshaw.

I overheard a bookseller near Nai Sarak recite a couplet of Ghalib to a young customer: “Bazm mein Ghalib ne jo suna, tum bhi suno.” The customer nodded, smiled, and Googled the next line. Chandni Chowk’s stories do not just survive—they adapt, they evolve, and they travel through time in whatever language people now understand.

Faces That Walk Between Eras

Perhaps the most telling sign that time is an illusion here is in the people who move through it. A man in a traditional sherwani steps out of an attar shop, adjusting his Apple Watch. A woman in a silk saree haggles over the price of a pashmina, but before sealing the deal, she sends a picture to her daughter studying in Boston. A young boy runs past, holding a paper kite, his laughter lost in the ringtone of a delivery agent waiting at a chai stall.

I once asked a spice trader how long he had been in business. He smiled, adjusting his thick glasses, and said, “Mere dada ke dada bhi yahi baithe the. Farq sirf yeh hai ki pehle yahan baithne ka waqt milta tha.” His great-grandfather sat in the same spot. The only difference? Back then, there was time to sit.

A City That Does Not Forget

Many cities erase their pasts to make way for the future. Delhi does not. It keeps its history alive—not as a monument, but as a pulse, a rhythm. The havelis still stand, their doors heavy with the weight of memories. Some have turned into boutique hotels, others into crumbling ruins that refuse to fall entirely. The past here is not a chapter that ended; it is a book still being written.

So, does time move in Chandni Chowk, or does it merely shift its shape? Maybe it does neither. Maybe, in a city that holds its past so close, time does the one thing it is not supposed to do.

Maybe, just maybe, time stays.

02 / Arts & society

Cultural Shifts: How the Arts Themselves Are Responding to Global Challenges

By Shikhar Pathak

As every aspect of life finds creative expression, it becomes evident that addressing society’s problems comes with a diverse toolset. Over the past few years, creators from around the globe have stepped beyond the confines of galleries and performance stages to grapple with some of the most significant issues of time: climate change, and social and political justice.

From streets adorned with vibrant murals to gallery walls showcasing impactful exhibits, art pulses with activism. Urban landscapes burst with murals illustrating melting ice caps, designed to grab the attention of those who rarely engage with environmental reports. In Europe, theater groups are breathing new life into political satire, employing humour and poetic devices to spark conversations on intricate issues like immigration and social welfare in our globalised world.

Music has also embraced this wave of change. The protest anthems of rap and folk resonate in diverse locations such as Jamaica and India, where artists leverage their voices not just for entertainment but to challenge societal norms. These songs, draped in familiar, warm harmonies, deliver lines that are sharp and unflinching. In India, a new wave of independent musicians is crafting “protest” music that draws on historical and contemporary struggles, from colonial oppression to the marginalisation of indigenous communities like the Bodos.

Film, a powerful visual medium, has also become a significant force for change. Movies like Don’t Look Up use satire and hyperbole to alert audiences to the dangers of ignorance and inaction regarding existential threats. Documentaries focusing on human rights and climate change have gained traction, serving not only as records of oppression but as rallying points for active engagement. These films aim to move beyond simply documenting injustice; they seek to forge solidarity and galvanise action among diverse communities.

Art, in all its forms, has embraced a new role—not just as an observer but as an active participant in the discourse of our time. No longer confined to quiet admiration or passive reflection, today’s art demands attention, action, and empathy. It urges us to not only see but to feel and respond, asking us to confront the realities of our world and engage with them meaningfully.

In literature, words take flight to provoke and comfort. Poets from Nigeria to the United States pen verses that trace the roots of modern civil rights movements, offering both elegy and rallying cry. The novelists of today don’t shy from drawing vivid parallels between fictional dystopias and real-world anxieties, prompting readers to confront uncomfortable truths.

No longer solely a retreat or an expression of beauty, the arts are now a force—a binding agent of collective consciousness. They prod society’s conscience, not just reflecting the times but, as James Baldwin once said, bearing “the burden of truth” and daring audiences to take up that challenge.

03 / Economy & public policy

India’s Economic Slowdown: A Wake-Up Call for Inclusive Growth

By Shikhar Pathak · New Delhi · January 31, 2025

India, long celebrated as a nation of high potential, now finds itself grappling with an unpleasant reality. Consumption, arguably the heart of our economy, is shrinking, and with it comes the worry of a slowdown. The latest projections for the 2024–25 financial year paint a worrying picture: growth is expected to slide to 6.4%, the slowest pace in four years.

This slowdown is not surprising. Private consumption, which reflects the health of an economy, is dwindling. Wages have barely moved while prices for essential goods have risen exceptionally. The common worker, bearing the burden, cannot afford to spend as freely as before. On the other hand, corporations, despite achieving record profits, have invested little in either job creation or wage hikes.

The result? A plateau in demand across sectors. Manufacturing growth is down to 6.2%, and Gross Fixed Capital Formation, a key indicator of investments, barely moves. Inflation, largely driven by food and energy costs, continues to pile pressure on families, shrinking their ability to spend and save.

What’s alarming is the lack of urgency to fix this. Corporate profits serve no purpose if they don’t flow back into the economy, whether through better pay for workers or investments in new industries. India’s growth cannot rest on numbers alone; it needs to touch lives.

To revive the economy, businesses must step up. They should expand capacities, create jobs, and most importantly, pay their workers better. The government too must step in, offering tax benefits for reinvestment and holding corporations accountable for hoarding wealth.
